#D 1
## The CIA triad
Almost every security idea traces back to three goals, remembered as the **CIA triad**:
- **Confidentiality** — information is seen only by those authorised. Achieved mainly with **encryption** and access controls.
- **Integrity** — information is accurate and unaltered. Verified with **hashing** and digital signatures; if a file's hash still matches, it hasn't changed.
- **Availability** — systems and data are usable when needed. Protected with **redundancy, backups, and DDoS defences**.
Every control you learn supports one or more of these. A denial-of-service attack harms availability; eavesdropping harms confidentiality; tampering harms integrity.

## Authentication, authorization, accounting (AAA)
Three related ideas that are easy to confuse:
- **Authentication** proves *who you are* (you log in).
- **Authorization** decides *what you may do* once identified (your permissions).
- **Accounting** records *what you did* (logs and audit trails).
Together they are called **AAA**. A fourth idea, **non-repudiation**, means someone cannot later deny an action — digital signatures provide it because only the signer holds the private key.

## Why the triad guides decisions
When you evaluate any control, ask which goal it serves. Full-disk encryption → confidentiality. A RAID array or load balancer → availability. A file hash or digital signature → integrity. This framing keeps security decisions grounded rather than reacting to fear — you spend effort where a real goal is at risk.

#D 2
## Control types (who enforces it)
Security **controls** are the safeguards you put in place. They are classified two ways. First, by **type** — the nature of the control:
- **Technical** (also called logical) — enforced by technology: firewalls, encryption, antivirus.
- **Managerial** (administrative) — the policies and procedures that direct the program: a security policy, risk assessments.
- **Operational** — carried out by people day to day: awareness training, an incident-response procedure.
- **Physical** — protect the tangible world: locks, fences, guards, cameras.

## Control functions (what it does)
Second, by **function** — what the control accomplishes:
- **Preventive** — stops an incident (a door lock, a firewall rule).
- **Detective** — notices an incident (an IDS, CCTV recording, a log alert).
- **Corrective** — fixes or recovers after one (a backup restore, re-imaging).
- **Deterrent** — discourages an attacker (a warning sign, visible cameras).
- **Compensating** — an alternative used when the primary control isn't feasible (isolating a legacy server you cannot patch).
- **Directive** — instructs behaviour (a policy telling staff what to do).
Any real control usually has both a type and a function — antivirus is *technical* and mostly *preventive/detective*.

## Defence in depth
No single control is perfect, so you layer them — **defence in depth**. If one layer fails, others still protect the asset. A compensating control fills a gap when the ideal control can't be used.

#D 3
## Change management
Unplanned or untracked changes are one of the biggest causes of outages **and** new vulnerabilities, so mature organisations control change formally. A good change process includes: a clear purpose, an assessment of **risk and impact**, a **test plan**, approval (often by a change advisory board), a **maintenance window** to make disruptive changes at a low-impact time, and — critically — a **rollback/backout plan** so a change can be undone if it breaks something. The rule of thumb: never deploy a change you cannot reverse. Version control (like Git) is a simple, powerful change-management tool for configurations.

## Zero trust
The old model trusted everything inside the corporate firewall. **Zero trust** removes that assumption: *never trust, always verify* — every request is authenticated and authorised regardless of where it comes from. Key pieces:
- The **Policy Decision Point (PDP)** decides whether to allow a request; the **Policy Enforcement Point (PEP)** actually enforces that decision.
- **Least privilege** — give each user or process only the access it needs, limiting the damage a compromised account can do.
- **Microsegmentation** — divide the network into small zones so a breach in one cannot spread freely.

## Why these matter together
Change management protects availability and integrity by keeping the environment predictable; zero trust protects confidentiality and integrity by refusing to assume any request is safe. Both reflect the same mindset: reduce blast radius and verify before trusting.

#D 4
## Threat actors
A **threat actor** is anyone who might attack you. They differ in skill, resources and motivation, and knowing the type helps you prepare:
- **Nation-state / APT (Advanced Persistent Threat)** — the best-funded and most patient; motivated by espionage or sabotage.
- **Organised crime** — motivated by **money**; runs ransomware and fraud.
- **Hacktivist** — motivated by a political or social **cause**; defaces sites or leaks data.
- **Script kiddie** — low skill, using tools written by others; still dangerous.
- **Insider threat** — a trusted employee causing harm, deliberately or accidentally; dangerous because they already have access.

## Actor attributes and motivations
Describe actors by **attributes**: internal vs external, their level of **sophistication/capability**, and their **resources/funding**. And by **motivation**: financial gain, espionage, political/philosophical belief, revenge, notoriety, or simple disruption. **Shadow IT** — hardware or software used without IT approval — creates unmanaged risk that any of these actors can exploit.

## Threat intelligence
**Threat intelligence** is knowledge about likely attackers and their techniques, shared through feeds and frameworks so defenders can prepare. **MITRE ATT&CK** is the widely used catalogue of adversary **tactics, techniques and procedures (TTPs)** — it gives red and blue teams a shared language for how attacks actually unfold.

#D 5
## What social engineering is
**Social engineering** attacks the *person* rather than the machine, tricking someone into revealing information or taking an unsafe action. It works by exploiting human tendencies — **authority, urgency, scarcity, familiarity and fear**. Because people are the target, the strongest defences are **awareness training** plus **verification procedures** and MFA.

## The main techniques
- **Phishing** — fraudulent messages (usually email) that trick users into clicking malicious links or revealing data. It is the most common initial attack vector.
- **Spear phishing** — phishing targeted at a specific person with personalised detail; **whaling** targets executives.
- **Vishing** — voice phishing (phone calls); **smishing** — phishing via SMS.
- **Pretexting** — inventing a believable scenario ("I'm from the fraud team…") to gain trust.
- **Watering hole** — compromising a website the target group is known to visit.
- **Business email compromise** — impersonating an executive to authorise a fraudulent payment.

## Physical and everyday tricks
- **Tailgating (piggybacking)** — following an employee through a secure door without badging in.
- **Shoulder surfing** — watching someone type a password.
- **Baiting** — leaving a malware-loaded USB stick for someone to plug in.
The right response to a suspected attack is simple: **don't click or reply, report it, and verify any request through a known official channel** — never the contact details in the suspicious message itself.

#D 6
## Malware families
**Malware** is malicious software. The types are distinguished by how they spread and what they do:
- **Virus** — attaches to a file and needs a user action to spread.
- **Worm** — spreads by itself across networks, no user needed.
- **Trojan** — disguised as legitimate software so the user runs it.
- **Ransomware** — encrypts or locks data and demands payment; best defended with **offline/immutable backups**, patching, MFA and least privilege.
- **Spyware / keylogger** — secretly gathers information or records keystrokes.
- **Rootkit** — hides itself and gains privileged, persistent control of the OS, making it hard to detect.
- **Logic bomb** — malicious code that triggers on a condition (a date, a user being deleted).

## Modern evasive malware
- **Fileless malware** runs in memory using legitimate tools (like PowerShell) instead of dropping a detectable file — "living off the land".
- A **botnet** is a network of compromised devices (bots/zombies) controlled by an attacker through a **command-and-control (C2)** server, often used for DDoS.

## Detecting malware
Behaviour is the giveaway: new scheduled tasks (persistence), a process with no file on disk (fileless), regular beacon traffic to a strange IP (C2 check-in), or a renamed system binary (evasion). Modern defence uses **EDR** (behaviour-based detection) rather than only signature antivirus, plus indicators of compromise (IOCs) like file hashes and malicious IPs.

#D 7
## Password attacks
Passwords are attacked in several ways:
- **Brute force** — trying every possible combination; slow, defeated by long passwords and lockouts.
- **Dictionary attack** — trying likely words from a wordlist.
- **Credential stuffing** — reusing username/password pairs leaked from one site against many others; works because people reuse passwords, and is stopped by MFA.
- **Password spraying** — trying one common password across many accounts to avoid per-account lockout.
- **Pass-the-hash** — authenticating with a stolen password hash without cracking it.
Defences: **MFA**, long passphrases, account lockout/rate limiting, and — on the server side — **salted, slow hashing** (bcrypt/argon2). A **salt** is random data added before hashing so identical passwords hash differently, defeating precomputed "rainbow tables".

## Application attacks
Attacks that abuse how software handles input:
- **SQL injection** — inserting malicious SQL through an input field the app runs against its database. Fixed with **parameterized queries** (prepared statements).
- **Cross-site scripting (XSS)** — injecting script that runs in another user's browser. Fixed with output encoding and a Content Security Policy.
- **Buffer overflow** — writing more data than a memory buffer holds, corrupting adjacent memory and possibly running attacker code; mitigated by ASLR/DEP.
- **Race condition (TOCTOU)** — exploiting the gap between the *time of check* and the *time of use*.
The common thread: never trust user input — validate it, and treat it as data, never as code.

#D 8
## On-path and spoofing attacks
Network attacks intercept or redirect traffic:
- **Man-in-the-middle / on-path** — the attacker sits between two parties, reading and possibly altering traffic. Defended with **TLS everywhere** and certificate validation.
- **ARP poisoning** — tricking devices on a local network into sending traffic to the attacker by faking the mapping of an IP to the attacker's MAC. Dynamic ARP Inspection helps.
- **DNS poisoning (spoofing)** — corrupting DNS so a legitimate name resolves to a malicious IP. DNSSEC and trusted resolvers reduce it.
- **Replay attack** — capturing valid data (like an auth token) and resending it; defeated by timestamps, nonces and session tokens.

## Denial of service
- **DoS** floods a service so real users cannot reach it; a **DDoS** does it from many compromised sources at once (a botnet). Cloud "scrubbing" services and rate limiting absorb the flood. A **SYN flood** exhausts a server's half-open connections; SYN cookies mitigate it.

## Wireless and encryption fixes
- A **rogue access point** is any unauthorised AP; an **evil twin** specifically impersonates a legitimate network's name (SSID) to trick users into connecting.
The overarching defence against sniffing and interception is **encryption in transit** (TLS/IPsec) and **network segmentation** — captured traffic that is encrypted is useless, and segmentation limits how far an intruder can move. Modern DNS privacy (DoH/DoT) encrypts lookups to stop on-path snooping.
