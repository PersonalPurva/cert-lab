#D 25
## What risk is
**Risk** is the chance that a threat exploits a vulnerability, and the impact if it does — roughly **likelihood × impact**. Security is really risk management: you cannot remove all risk, so you decide which risks to reduce and which to accept.

## The four risk responses
For each risk you choose one of:
- **Mitigate** — add controls to reduce likelihood or impact.
- **Transfer** — shift the impact to someone else (buying cyber-insurance).
- **Avoid** — stop doing the risky activity.
- **Accept** — knowingly live with the residual risk.
**Residual risk** is what remains after controls are applied; leadership formally accepts it. A **risk register** is a living document listing each risk, its owner, and its treatment status.

## Measuring risk
- **Qualitative** analysis rates risk high/medium/low by judgement.
- **Quantitative** analysis uses numbers:
  - **SLE (Single Loss Expectancy)** = asset value × exposure factor.
  - **ARO (Annualized Rate of Occurrence)** = how many times per year it's expected.
  - **ALE (Annualized Loss Expectancy)** = SLE × ARO.
Example: a $20,000 asset, 25% destroyed, once every 4 years → SLE = $5,000, ARO = 0.25, ALE = $1,250/year. A control costing less than $1,250/year that removes this risk is justified.

## Why third-party risk matters
Your security is only as strong as the vendors who touch your data — a weakness in one of them can become **your** breach (supply-chain risk). So you assess vendors before and during the relationship.

## Agreements that manage the relationship
- **SLA (Service Level Agreement)** — the measurable service levels a vendor must meet (uptime, response times); include security requirements and penalties.
- **MOU/MOA** — a (often non-binding) statement of intentions between parties.
- **BPA (Business Partners Agreement)** — governs responsibilities and liability between business partners.
- A **right-to-audit** clause lets you verify a vendor's controls yourself or via a third party — without it you rely only on their word.

## Assurance and lifecycle
- A **SOC 2** report gives independent assurance about a service provider's security/availability controls.
- **Vendor lock-in** is a risk because leaving becomes costly — plan an exit strategy and data portability up front.
- **End-of-life (EOL)** vendor products stop getting security updates, so plan replacement before EOL.
For a software dependency, reduce supply-chain risk by pinning versions, verifying checksums/signatures, using a private/proxy registry, and scanning for CVEs before upgrading.

#D 27
## Assessments vs audits vs testing
Different activities verify security in different ways:
- A **vulnerability scan** identifies weaknesses automatically but does **not** exploit them.
- A **penetration test** goes further, **exploiting** flaws to prove real impact within an authorised scope.
- An **audit** independently verifies that controls exist and work against a standard or policy; **attestation** is a formal statement that they were reviewed and meet criteria.
- A **compliance scan** checks systems against a required benchmark (PCI DSS, CIS).

## Penetration-test knowledge
- **Black box** = the tester has no internal knowledge; **white box** = full knowledge and credentials; **gray box** = partial.
- **Rules of engagement** define the authorised scope, timing, allowed techniques and contacts — testing outside them can be illegal.
- A **red team** simulates a real adversary to test detection and response; the **blue team** defends; **purple** is the two collaborating.

## Continuous validation
- A **bug bounty** program pays external researchers to responsibly report vulnerabilities under defined rules, widening your testing pool.
- Retesting after remediation confirms fixes actually worked. The point of all of this is to find and fix weaknesses *before* an attacker does — and prioritise by real risk, not raw counts.

#D 28
## Automation and orchestration in security
Automation runs security tasks consistently, quickly and repeatably, reducing human error — for example **SOAR** playbooks that auto-triage and contain common incidents. But automation cuts both ways: a flawed script can cause damage at scale, so it needs guardrails, approvals and testing. **Idempotent** automation (running it repeatedly yields the same safe end state) is the goal, as with configuration tools like Ansible.

## Secure development and pipelines
- **Shift left** means adding security testing early in the development pipeline (CI/CD) — catching flaws with SAST/DAST and dependency scans *before* production, which is far cheaper than fixing them later.
- **Sign your scripts and artifacts** so only trusted, untampered code runs — a supply-chain defence; verification stops a tampered artifact from deploying.

## Handling secrets and service accounts
- Never store secrets (API keys, passwords) in code — anyone with the code gets them. Use a **secrets manager** and inject them at runtime.
- Automation **service accounts** must follow least privilege and be monitored; an over-privileged automation account is a prime target. Scanning code for hard-coded secrets before committing is a simple, high-value habit.

#D 29
## Endpoint protection
Endpoints (laptops, servers, phones) are where users work and where attacks often land, so they get layered protection:
- **Antivirus** catches known-signature malware.
- **EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response)** goes further — continuous monitoring of behaviour and telemetry, with the ability to investigate and contain remotely. It catches suspicious *behaviour*, not just known files.
- **Application allow-listing** blocks unknown executables from running.
- **HIPS** (host intrusion prevention) blocks malicious behaviour on the host itself.
The strong anti-ransomware combination is EDR + least privilege + patching + tested offline backups.

## Email security
Email is the top delivery route for attacks, so it gets dedicated defences:
- **SPF, DKIM and DMARC** together verify a sender's authenticity and reduce spoofing/phishing — SPF authorises sending servers, DKIM signs messages, DMARC sets policy and reporting.
- **Sandboxing** detonates a suspicious attachment in an isolated environment to observe its behaviour safely.
- **Content Disarm and Reconstruction (CDR)** strips active content (macros/scripts) and rebuilds a safe version of a file.
Combined with user awareness training and MFA, these blunt both the delivery and the payoff of email attacks.

#D 30
## Approaching the SY0-701 exam
The Security+ exam is up to **90 questions in 90 minutes**, mixing multiple-choice with **performance-based questions (PBQs)** — small hands-on scenarios like configuring a firewall rule, reading logs, or matching controls. The passing score is **750 on a 100–900 scale** (so not a simple percentage).

## Strategy
- PBQs take longer, so a common tactic is to **flag** them, answer the quick multiple-choice questions first to bank those points, then return.
- Watch for keywords: **"BEST"** and **"MOST likely"** signal that more than one answer looks correct — rank them and pick the strongest, most secure, most directly relevant option.
- There's **no penalty for guessing**, so never leave a blank — eliminate wrong options and make your best choice.

## What the exam really tests
Security+ rewards understanding *concepts* and choosing the *best* control for a situation, not memorising trivia. The PBQs reward hands-on familiarity — reading a log, picking firewall rules, hardening a config — which is exactly why practising the labs and command drills alongside the theory pays off. Think like a defender: which option most reduces real risk?
